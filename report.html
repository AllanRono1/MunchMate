Nutrition for Immuno‑Compromised Patients

Clinical reference – practical, evidence‑based, and tailored to Kenyan dietary contexts

1. Introduction

Immunocompromised individuals have diminished capacity to mount effective immune responses, making them highly susceptible to infections, delayed wound healing, and nutrient‑mediated complications. 1‑3 Adequate nutrition is therefore a therapeutic cornerstone: it supplies macro‑ and micronutrients that are essential for immune cell proliferation, antibody production, and mucosal barrier integrity. In this population, nutrition must be personalised—accounting for age, gender, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, chronic kidney disease), polypharmacy, food allergies, and socioeconomic factors such as food access and cost.

3. Renal & Hepatic Dietary Restrictions

Condition Restriction Practical Implementation (Kenyan context)
Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) – Stage 3‑5 ↓ phosphorus (≤ 800 mg/d), ↓ potassium (≤ 2 g/d), moderate protein (0.8–1.0 g/kg BW) Choose low‑phosphorus cereals (white maize meal), limit beans & nuts; use boiled plantain or rice; avoid processed meats high in phosphates.
End‑Stage Renal Disease (on dialysis) ↑ protein quality (essential amino acids), ↑ sodium‑restriction (≤ 2 g/d) Include dairy‑free protein sources (eg, boiled lentils, tofu – boiled 5 min), use low‑salt broth, monitor fluid intake.
Acute Hepatic Insufficiency ↑ high‑quality protein (prefer plant‑based), ↓ copper, ↓ iron overload Use millet porridge, boiled sweet potato, limited red meat; avoid raw liver; ensure adequate zinc (10 mg/d) to support synthesis of albumin.

4. Drug‑Nutrient Interaction Table

Drug / Class Typical Interaction Partner Effect on Drug / Nutrient Clinical Advice
CYP3A4 substrates (e.g., tacrolimus, cyclosporine, some tyrosine‑kinase inhibitors) Grapefruit, St John’s Wort, Ritonavir (inhibitor) Inhibitors ↑ bioavailability → risk of toxicity; inducers ↓ exposure → sub‑therapeutic levels Avoid grapefruit juice; separate St John’s Wort ≥ 2 h from dosing; therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) required.
Vitamin K‑rich foods (kale, spinach) Warfarin ↑ Vitamin K → ↓ anticoagulant effect → higher INR variability Keep vitamin K intake consistent; compile a food‑log; consider INR checks every 2‑3 days after diet change.
Tyramine‑rich foods (aged cheese, fermented sausages) MAO‑inhibitors (e.g., phenelzine) Risk of hypertensive crisis Strict avoidance of high‑tyramine foods; educate patients.
Potassium‑rich foods (banana, sweet potato) ACE‑inhibitors (lisinopril), ARBs (losartan) Hyperkalaemia risk Monitor serum K⁺; limit to ≤ 2 g/d; adjust dose if needed.
Calcium‑binding antibiotics (tetracycline, quinolone) High‑calcium foods (milk, dairy) Reduced absorption → sub‑therapeutic antibacterial levels Separate administration by ≥ 2 h (e.g., take med with water 1 h before dairy).
Selenium (Brazil nuts, fish) Chemotherapy agents (e.g., platinum‑based) Potential antagonism → increased oxidative stress Limit to ≤ 100 µg/d; monitor renal function.
Iron (red meat, fortified cereals) Calcium supplements, antacids Iron absorption ↓ Take iron on empty stomach; avoid calcium ≥ 2 h after.

References: Nutrition Today (2026) drug‑interaction review; UpToDate™ interaction database; FDA label monographs.

(Designed for a 70‑kg, 68‑yr‑old man on warfarin, dialysis‑naïve Stage 3 CKD, and on tacrolimus)

Meal Foods (≈ 300–400 kcal, balanced macronutrients) Portion Key Nutrients
Breakfast Fortified milky tea (pasteurised milk 150 ml + ½ tsp Milo + 1 slice whole‑grain maziwa toast) + 1 boiled egg (well‑cooked) 1 bowl Protein ≈ 18 g, calcium ≈ 250 mg, vitamin D ≈ 120 IU, low potassium
Mid‑morning Snack Small portion of roasted peanuts (unsalted) 30 g Zinc ≈ 1 mg, selenium ≈ 10 µg
Lunch Chicken stew – boiled chicken thigh (150 g), karanga (pumpkin leaves) cooked with low‑sodium broth, white maize meal (ugali) (½ cup), side of boiled carrots 1 plate Protein ≈ 35 g, calcium ≈ 150 mg, vitamin A ≈ 600 µg, fibre ≈ 4 g
Afternoon Snack Plain yoghurt (pasteurised) + sliced apple (washed) 150 ml + ½ apple Protein ≈ 5 g, calcium ≈ 150 mg, probiotic
Dinner Grilled tilapia (100 g), sukuma wiki lightly boiled (½ cup), brown rice (½ cup), tea with skimmed milk 1 plate Protein ≈ 25 g, omega‑3 ≈ 800 mg, potassium ≈ 200 mg (controlled), vitamin C ≈ 70 mg
Evening Water (150 ml) + herbal suji tea (no added sugar) – Hydration

All vegetables washed in running water ≥ 30 s; eggs boiled ≥ 9 min; meat cooked to ≥ 74 °C internal temperature.

6. Conclusion – Key Safety Takeaways

  • Personalise every plan: age, comorbidities, medication list, allergies, and local food availability dictate nutrient targets.
  • Avoid raw/undercooked animal products, unpasteurised dairy, and unwashed produce.
  • Monitor drug‑nutrient interactions, especially those involving CYP3A4 substrates, vitamin K, potassium, calcium/iron timing, and St John’s Wort.
  • Maintain consistent intake of zinc, selenium, omega‑3, and antioxidants while respecting disease‑specific restrictions (e.g., low‑phosphorus in CKD).
  • Engage a registered dietitian for periodic assessment, dose adjustments, and education on label reading and food‑preparation safety.
